Backup Sump Pump in Midvale, UT: Which Type to Install and How It Works

A sump pump fails at the worst time, usually in a storm, when the power is out or the float is stuck and the pit is already full. A backup pump is the second line of defense that keeps water out of the basement when the first one quits.

This article explains the three common backup types, how an install works, what a homeowner can handle, and which Midvale resources help when water is already coming in.

The question first

Do I need a backup sump pump?

If your basement has a sump pit, a backup is worth considering for one reason. A single pump is a single point of failure. Pumps wear out, floats stick, switches fail, and storms often take the power out in the same hour that the pit needs it most.

A backup matters more if the basement is finished, if you store things below grade, or if the pit fills in minutes during heavy rain. If the pit rarely runs, a backup is cheaper than the carpet you would otherwise replace.

Why they fail

Four ways a primary sump pump stops working

Knowing the failure mode helps you pick the backup that actually covers it.

Power outage

A normal pump needs household power. When the grid drops during a storm, the pump stops and the pit keeps filling.

Stuck float switch

The float can wedge against the pit wall or a pipe and never tell the motor to start. The pump is fine, but it never runs.

Clogged intake or check valve

Silt and gravel clog the intake. A stuck check valve lets water run back into the pit after every cycle.

Age and overwork

A pump that runs every few minutes for weeks wears out faster. Many last 7 to 10 years under normal duty.

Backup pump types compared

Three setups cover most homes. The right one depends on your power, your water pressure and how much flood risk you accept.

How a backup sump pump installation works

The details vary with the pit. This is the usual order.

  1. 1

    Check the pit

    Most pits are about 18 inches wide and 22 to 24 inches deep. A second pump needs room beside the first or its own pit.

  2. 2

    Set the backup lower or higher

    The backup float is placed a few inches above the main pump float so it runs only if the main one fails.

  3. 3

    Plumb the discharge

    Each pump gets its own check valve and tees into the discharge pipe, often 1.5 inches wide.

  4. 4

    Connect power

    Battery units need a charger on a protected outlet. A second pump needs its own GFCI outlet.

  5. 5

    Test under load

    Pour about 5 gallons into the pit and watch both pumps run through a full cycle.

Monthly sump pump test routine

This takes five minutes and finds most failures before a storm does.

  1. Pour 3 to 5 gallons of water into the pit and watch the float lift
  2. Confirm the pump turns on, empties the pit and shuts off
  3. Listen for grinding, rattling or a motor that hums without pumping
  4. Check that the discharge pipe is clear and ends away from the foundation
  5. Unplug the main pump and see whether the backup starts
  6. Inspect the battery terminals and charger light on a battery system

Write the test date on the pit lid. If the pit holds standing water that does not drain, or the pump runs without stopping, call a plumber.

DIY install or licensed plumber?

Adding a backup is a common weekend project, but the plumbing and electrical details matter.

A confident homeowner can often

  • Test and clean the existing pump
  • Swap a bad float switch
  • Add a plug-in battery unit beside the pump
  • Clear debris from the pit and intake

Better handled by a pro

  • Cutting into the discharge line and adding a second check valve
  • Making a new pit in a concrete slab
  • Wiring a dedicated outlet
  • Finding why the pit fills so fast
  • Fixing a discharge that sends water back toward the house
