DIY Basement Waterproofing: What Works, What Does Not, and When to Stop

Water gets into a basement in a few predictable ways: it runs toward the house across the soil, it pours off the roof beside the foundation, or it rises from the ground under the floor. The cheapest fixes deal with the first two and you can do most of them yourself in a weekend.

This guide goes in order, from the outside in, and says plainly where DIY stops. We also cover what Midvale City says about flooding and where to get sandbags if a storm is on the way.

Start here

Find out where the water is coming from

Fixing the wrong cause wastes a weekend. Spend an hour on diagnosis first.

  1. 1

    Do the foil test

    Tape a 2-foot square of foil to the wall or floor. Check it after two days. Moisture on the outside means seepage, on the inside means condensation.

  2. 2

    Note the timing

    Water after rain or snowmelt points to the outside. Water in dry weather points to a plumbing leak.

  3. 3

    Look at the spot

    Cracks, the joint where wall meets floor and window wells are the usual entry points.

If water appears with no rain and the meter moves with everything off, you may have a plumbing leak instead. Our leak detection crew can sort that out.

Outside first

The cheap fixes that stop most basement water

These do the most good for the least money.

Fix the grading

Soil should slope away from the foundation, about 6 inches of drop over the first 10 feet. Fill low spots with compacted soil, not mulch.

Extend the downspouts

A roof sheds a lot of water in a storm. Run downspouts at least 5 to 10 feet from the wall with an extension or a buried drain.

Clean the gutters

A clogged gutter pours water down the wall. Clear them before fall storms and before snowmelt season.

Cover window wells

A clear well cover keeps rain out. Make sure the well has gravel or a drain at the bottom.

Midvale flooding

Midvale's stormwater page and the sandbag shed

The City of Midvale's stormwater page says residents are responsible for protecting their own property in a flood. The city does not promise to keep water out of your basement, so your own grading, gutters and window wells matter.

Salt Lake County runs a free sandbag shed, the Flood Control Sandbag Shed, at 604 West 6960 South in Midvale. It is open seven days a week from 8am to 6pm. Bring a shovel, since you fill the bags yourself.

If you see a spill or illegal discharge going into a storm drain, report it to Midvale Public Works at 801-567-7235. That line also handles water emergencies. Sandbags are useful at doors, window wells and low entry points when heavy rain or snowmelt is forecast, but they are a short-term barrier and not a repair.

Interior sealer or exterior drainage

Paint-on products are everywhere. Know what they can and cannot do.

DIY interior sealer

  • Fine for damp walls and minor seepage
  • Fails when water pressure pushes behind it
  • Cannot fix a leak at the floor and wall joint
  • Cheap and a one-day job

Pro drainage and sump systems

  • Collects water before it enters the room
  • Handles groundwater that rises
  • Can include a sump pit and pump
  • Needs digging or cutting the floor

A weekend DIY waterproofing checklist

Work in this order. Each step reduces what the next one has to deal with.

  1. Clean gutters and add downspout extensions
  2. Regrade low spots next to the foundation
  3. Seal small cracks in the wall with hydraulic cement
  4. Install clear covers on window wells
  5. Apply a masonry sealer to damp walls only
  6. Run a dehumidifier to keep humidity under 50 percent

Hydraulic cement sets in a few minutes, so mix small batches. Chisel a crack into a dovetail shape so the cement locks in instead of popping out.

Sump pumps and drains

A sump pump sits in a pit and pushes water out of the basement before it rises over the floor. If your home has one, test it a few times a year by pouring a bucket of water into the pit. It should start, pump out and shut off.

A pump is only as good as its power. A storm that knocks out electricity also stops the pump, so a battery backup is worth asking about. Our sump pump repair page covers repairs and testing.

When to call a pro

Stop the DIY and call a licensed plumber if any of these apply.

  • Water is coming up through the floor drain or from a toilet
  • The sump pump has failed during a storm
  • You smell sewage
  • Water is near the electrical panel or outlets

Permits for basement work

Midvale Building and Safety lists basement finishes among the work that needs a building permit and inspections. Simple waterproofing like sealing a crack or adding a downspout extension does not involve a finish, but any plan to finish the basement or add drains should be checked with them first at 801-567-7200 extension 1016.
