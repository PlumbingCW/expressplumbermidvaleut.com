Dripping Faucet Repair: Find the Cause, Fix the Part, Stop the Waste

A dripping faucet is one of the most common plumbing complaints, and one of the most fixable. The drip comes from a worn rubber or ceramic part inside the faucet that no longer seals, and replacing it takes about 30 minutes.

The hard part is knowing which kind of faucet you have, because each type uses a different part. Below we sort that out, walk through the repair, and cover the cases where the faucet itself is the problem.

The quick answer

What makes a faucet drip

Inside every faucet is a seal that presses against a seat to block water. Over the years that seal wears, the seat pits, or mineral deposits roughen the surface. Water then slips past even when the handle is off.

A drip from the spout means the seal inside is failing. A drip around the handle base means an O-ring or packing is worn. A leak under the sink points to the supply lines or a connection, not the faucet's moving parts.

Which type of faucet do you have?

Identify the faucet by its handles and how it moves. That tells you which part to buy.

Take a photo before you disassemble anything.

If you cannot tell what you have, the brand and model are often stamped on the base or under the sink. Take the old part to a hardware store so the clerk can match it.

How to repair a dripping faucet

The sequence is the same for most types. Only the part changes.

  1. 1

    Shut off the water

    Close the two valves under the sink, then open the faucet to drain it. Plug the drain so small screws cannot fall in.

  2. 2

    Remove the handle

    Pop off the cap, undo the screw and lift the handle. Penetrating oil helps if it sticks.

  3. 3

    Pull the stem or cartridge

    Unscrew the retaining nut with an adjustable wrench and lift out the working part.

  4. 4

    Replace the worn pieces

    Swap washers, O-rings or the whole cartridge. Coat new rubber with plumber's grease.

  5. 5

    Reassemble and test

    Put everything back in order, open the shutoffs slowly and check for drips.

Keep every old part until the new one works. If the faucet is pitted around the seat, the repair may not hold and replacement is the better path.

Midvale water

A drip on a metered Midvale line

Every water connection in Midvale is metered, and the city reads the meters monthly with radio-read devices. Monthly scans flag continuous flow, so a drip that never stops contributes to a pattern the city is set up to notice, along with the usage you pay for.

The EPA says a faucet dripping once per second can waste more than 3,000 gallons a year. That is an estimate, not a measurement, and the real volume depends on how large the drops are and how fast they fall. Even a slower drip runs all day and night.

If you suspect more than a faucet, check your meter. Turn off every fixture, then watch the leak indicator or the dial. If it keeps moving, you have a leak somewhere, and a leak detection visit can find it. Midvale Public Utilities at 801-567-7239 handles leak and pressure questions Monday to Thursday.

Other drips

Drips that are not from the spout

Water in the wrong place is not always the faucet.

Under the sink

Check the supply lines and shutoff valves with a dry paper towel. A damp towel after 10 minutes means a connection is weeping.

Around the handle

An O-ring or packing nut is worn. This is a quick fix on most faucets and usually does not need a new faucet.

From the base

A loose mounting nut or a cracked body lets water seep below the deck. If the body is cracked, replacement is the only fix.

From the shower or tub spout

A tub or shower drip often comes from the valve behind the wall. That is a bigger job and needs opening the wall.

Repair the faucet or replace it

A faucet that is old, pitted or discontinued sometimes costs more to patch than to swap.

Repair makes sense when

  • The faucet body is sound with no corrosion
  • Parts are easy to find at a hardware store
  • The faucet is fairly new
  • The drip started recently

Replace makes sense when

  • The body is corroded or cracked
  • Parts are discontinued or hard to match
  • The finish is peeling
  • You have repaired the same drip twice

Hard water, scale and slow drips

Mineral scale is a common reason a new washer fails early. Water supplied by the Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District averaged 182.4 mg/L total hardness in 2025, which the USGS counts as very hard. That is the east side of Midvale. West-side homes are on city wells, so ask the utility for its water quality report.

Scale builds on the seat and roughens it, so a fresh rubber washer sits on a bad surface and leaks again. If you have crusty white deposits, soak the aerator and parts in vinegar for an hour, then scrub with an old toothbrush. A seat that is deeply pitted may need a reseating tool or a new faucet.

When to call a pro

A drip is rarely urgent, but some faucet problems are. Call a licensed plumber when you see any of these.

  • Water will not shut off even with the handle closed
  • The shutoff valves under the sink are stuck or leak when turned
  • Water is coming through the ceiling or wall below the fixture
  • You have replaced the part and it still drips
