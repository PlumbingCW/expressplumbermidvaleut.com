How to Install a Toilet Fill Valve (and What a Running Toilet Costs You in Midvale)

If your toilet hisses, refills on its own or never quite stops running, the fill valve is the usual suspect. It is the tall part on the left side of the tank, and replacing it is a job most homeowners can finish in under an hour with a few basic tools.

This guide covers how to confirm the diagnosis, how to swap the valve, and where it goes wrong. It also explains why a stuck valve matters more in Midvale than you might think, because the city watches for continuous water flow.

Diagnose first

Is it the fill valve, or the flapper?

A running toilet has two common causes, and they look alike. If water leaks from the tank into the bowl, the flapper at the bottom is not sealing. If water keeps entering the tank and spills down the overflow tube, the fill valve is not shutting off.

Take the lid off and look. A tank level above the top of the overflow tube points to the fill valve. A tank level that sits normal but drops slowly points to the flapper.

The EPA suggests a dye test for silent leaks. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait 10 minutes without flushing, and see if color appears in the bowl. If it does, the flapper is leaking and a new fill valve will not fix it.

Tools and parts to gather before you start

Most home centers carry universal fill valves that fit nearly any tank. Check these before you shut off the water.

  • A universal fill valve sized for your tank height
  • A new braided supply line, 3/8 inch compression, if the old one is stiff or crusty
  • An adjustable wrench or channel-lock pliers
  • A small sponge and a towel for the last inch of water
  • A bucket and a flat screwdriver

A new supply line costs little and is worth replacing at the same time. Old lines get brittle at the connection and are one of the more common hidden leaks under a toilet.

Replace the fill valve in five moves

Take your time and keep the towel handy.

  1. 1

    Shut off and drain

    Close the wall valve, flush, and sponge out the remaining water in the tank.

  2. 2

    Remove the old valve

    Loosen the supply nut, unscrew the locknut under the tank and lift the valve straight out.

  3. 3

    Seat the new valve

    Set it in the hole, hand-tighten the locknut and set the height as the instructions say.

  4. 4

    Reconnect and clip

    Attach the supply line, then clip the refill tube to the overflow tube.

  5. 5

    Test

    Open the valve slowly, check every joint, flush twice and watch the shutoff level.

Hand-tight plus a quarter turn is enough on the plastic nuts. A cracked tank from overtightening is the most common way this repair goes wrong.

Getting the water level and height right

Every fill valve has a marked critical level line. The top of the valve must sit above the top of the overflow tube by the distance the instructions give, usually about 1 inch. Too low and the valve can siphon tank water back into the supply. Too high and it may never shut off.

Set the water level about 1 inch below the top of the overflow tube. If the valve has an adjustment screw or a float clip, use it in small steps and flush between each change.

The refill tube should aim into the overflow tube and stop above the water line. If the end of the tube dips into the tank water, it can pull water back out through the valve.

Midvale water use

Why a stuck fill valve gets noticed in Midvale

Midvale City meters every connection, reads them monthly and has replaced all its meters with radio-read units over the past ten years. The city's water conservation plan says monthly scans of the meter data flag continuous flow and unusually high usage.

A fill valve that never fully closes can keep a trickle moving all day. That is the kind of constant flow a monthly scan is designed to catch, and it also shows up on your own bill as water you never meant to use.

If you get a notice or see an odd bill, check the toilet first. Look at the tank, listen for hissing and do the dye test. Do it yourself or call a plumber

A fill valve is a good first repair. Some situations are better handled by a professional.

Good DIY candidate

  • Standard two-piece or one-piece toilet
  • Wall shutoff valve turns freely
  • Tank is intact with no cracks
  • You have an hour and basic tools

Call a licensed plumber

  • Wall valve is seized or leaks when turned
  • Water is leaking from the floor or the base
  • The tank has cracks or loose bolts
  • You see mineral build-up that blocks the inlet

Signs the problem is bigger than the fill valve

If a new valve does not stop the running, the issue may be a worn flapper or a flush valve seat that has corroded. Water that seems to disappear with no sound can also come from a leak elsewhere in the house. A leak detection visit finds hidden leaks that a tank repair never will.
