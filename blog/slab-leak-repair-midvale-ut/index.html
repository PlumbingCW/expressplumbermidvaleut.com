Slab Leak Repair: Signs, Tests and Repair Options

A slab leak is a water line that leaks under or inside the concrete floor of a house. You cannot see it, so it usually shows up as a bill that jumped, a floor that feels warm, or a sound of running water when everything is off.

This guide shows how to confirm one with a simple meter test, what repair involves, and which signs mean you should call right away. It also covers why Midvale's aging pipe is worth thinking about.

Warning signs

How to tell if you have a slab leak

One sign can mean many things. Two or three together make a slab leak much more likely.

Warm spot on the floor

A hot water line leaking under the slab warms the concrete. You may feel it on bare feet or see it in a carpet that stays warm.

Running water sound

Hissing or trickling when every fixture is off often comes from a pressurized line leaking under the floor.

High water bill

A bill that climbs with no change in habits is a classic clue. A leak runs 24 hours a day.

Damp floor or musty smell

Moisture under the flooring shows up as warped boards, damp carpet edges, mold or a smell like wet cardboard.

Low pressure

Water escaping under the slab can lower pressure at faucets, especially hot taps.

Cracks or shifting

Leaking water can erode soil under the slab. New cracks in walls or tile are a warning to check.

A simple meter test you can do in 15 minutes

Do this before calling anyone. It tells you whether water is moving when it should not be.

  1. 1

    Shut everything off

    Turn off all fixtures, the ice maker and the irrigation, and check that no toilet is running.

  2. 2

    Read the meter

    Open the meter box and note the dial or the small leak triangle. If it spins, water is moving.

  3. 3

    Check again

    Wait 15 minutes without using water. If the reading moved, you have a leak somewhere.

A moving meter does not prove the slab is the culprit. A toilet flapper, a sprinkler valve or a yard line can do the same thing. That is why a technician uses sound and heat tools to narrow it down.

Midvale pipes

What Midvale's old pipes and meter scans mean for you

Midvale's own water conservation plan describes many distribution pipes in the city as old, undersized and possibly leaking. The city replaces galvanized service laterals with copper or poly when they leak or when a pipeline project reaches them. It aims to replace about 1,000 feet of pipeline each year as budget allows.

For you, that matters in two ways. The pipe from the meter to your house is generally the property owner's responsibility, and older homes may still have galvanized or aging lines there. Galvanized steel corrodes from the inside and eventually pinholes, which is how many leaks start.

Midvale also reads every meter monthly with radio-read devices, and monthly scans flag continuous flow and high usage. If you get a notice or see a jump on your bill, run the meter test above. Leak and pressure questions go to Midvale Public Utilities at 801-567-7239, open Monday to Thursday. A water emergency after hours goes to Public Works at 801-567-7235.

How a slab leak is found

Jackhammering blind is a bad plan. A technician starts with an acoustic listening device that picks up the sound of escaping water through concrete. Thermal imaging can show the warm path of a hot water line. Pressure tests isolate which part of the system is losing water.

Once the line is located, the plumber marks the floor and explains the choices before any work begins. That keeps the damage to the smallest area that does the job.

Slab leak repair options compared

The right fix depends on how many leaks there are, how old the pipe is and where it runs.

Ask which option fits your pipe material and layout.
OptionWhat it involvesBest for
Spot repairBreak the slab, cut out the bad section, splice in new pipeOne leak in otherwise sound pipe
RerouteAbandon the slab line and run new pipe through walls or the atticRepeated leaks or hard-to-reach lines
RepipeReplace the whole system with new pipeOld pipe leaking in several places
Pipe liningCoat the inside of the line with a new linerSome lines where access is limited

If several leaks show up, a full repiping job may cost less over time than repeated spot repairs. We explain what the camera and tests show before you decide.

DIY checks or a licensed plumber

You can gather information. You should not open a slab.

What you can do yourself

  • Read the meter and do the 15-minute test
  • Listen near walls and floors for hissing
  • Feel the floor for warm spots
  • Take photos and note when symptoms started

What needs a licensed plumber

  • Pinpointing a leak under concrete
  • Breaking or cutting into the slab
  • Rerouting or replacing supply lines
  • Pulling any permits the repair needs

When a slab leak needs a call right now

Most slab leaks are slow. A few are not, and they get worse by the hour.

  • Water is bubbling up through the floor or a crack
  • A hot water line is leaking and the floor feels hot
  • Your meter is spinning fast with everything off
  • Flooring is swelling or flooding near electrical outlets

What to do while you wait

Turn off the main water valve, usually where the line enters the house or at the meter box. That stops the leak and keeps the slab from taking on more water. Open a faucet to relieve pressure.

Keep people and pets off wet floors, and stay away from outlets and cords near the water. Take photos of damage for your insurance company. Policies differ on slab leaks, so read yours or ask your agent about coverage for the repair and the water damage.
