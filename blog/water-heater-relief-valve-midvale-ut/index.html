Water Heater Relief Valve in Midvale, UT: What It Does, How to Test It and When to Replace It

The relief valve on your water heater is the part that stops a tank from becoming a pressure vessel with nowhere to vent. Most people never touch it. Then one day it drips, or hisses, or a plumber says to test it, and the questions begin.

This guide explains what the valve does, how to test it safely, how to read a drip, and what a correct discharge pipe looks like.

What it is

What does a water heater relief valve do?

A water heater relief valve is formally called a temperature and pressure relief valve, or T&P valve. It sits on the side or top of the tank with a short lever. If the water inside gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high, a spring opens the valve and releases water so the tank does not rupture.

Most are rated around 150 psi and 210 degrees Fahrenheit, which is above normal operating levels. Household water usually sits near 120 degrees and 40 to 80 psi. When the valve opens, something is wrong, and the valve is doing exactly the job it was built for.

When a relief valve is an emergency

A valve that blows steaming water or does not stop flowing needs attention now. Hot water at that temperature can scald in seconds.

Shut off power at the breaker for an electric heater, or turn the gas control to pilot or off for a gas heater, then call us. If you ever smell gas, leave the building right away and call Enbridge Gas at 1-800-767-1689 or 911 from a safe place.

  • Water spraying or steaming from the valve or discharge pipe
  • Rumbling or banging from the tank before the valve opens
  • A discharge pipe that is hot to the touch all the time
  • Water pooling under the tank with no obvious leak at the fittings

How to test a relief valve safely

A quick annual test shows whether the valve moves and reseats. Wear shoes and keep your hands and face clear of the discharge.

  1. 1

    Confirm the discharge pipe

    Make sure the pipe is attached and points down toward a floor or drain, never at people.

  2. 2

    Place a bucket

    Set a bucket beneath the open end because the first water out may be very hot.

  3. 3

    Lift the lever briefly

    Raise it for one or two seconds, then release. You should hear a rush of water and then a snap shut.

  4. 4

    Watch for dripping

    If water keeps trickling after the lever drops, the valve may not be seating and likely needs replacing.

If the lever is stuck or crusted shut, do not force it. A corroded valve is the one most likely to fail when needed, and a plumber can replace it.

Why a relief valve drips and what to do

A drip does not always mean a bad valve. The cause decides the fix.

Always find the cause. Replacing the valve alone can hide a pressure or temperature problem.
What you seeLikely causeTypical fix
Steady drip, the tank is hotThermostat set too high or failedLower the setting to about 120 degrees, call if it persists
Drips only when the heater is heatingThermal expansion with no reliefCheck for an expansion tank
Constant drip all dayWorn valve seat or mineral buildupReplace the valve
Spits water after a hot showerHigh house water pressureTest pressure with a gauge at a hose bib
Drip stops after you lift the leverDebris on the seatWatch for return, replace if it comes back

Discharge pipe: safe setup versus common mistakes

The valve is only safe if the water has a clear, safe path out. Codes vary, so confirm details locally.

Common mistakes

  • Capping or plugging the pipe so it never drips
  • Threading the end of the pipe
  • Running the pipe up or flat
  • Adding a shutoff valve on the discharge line
  • Using thin plastic that cannot take hot water

Safe setup

  • Pipe as large as the valve outlet, rated for hot water
  • Runs downhill the whole way
  • Ends a few inches above the floor or a drain
  • Open end visible and unthreaded
  • No valves, caps or plugs anywhere on the line

Midvale note

Permits when a relief valve problem leads to a new water heater

Midvale Building and Safety says water heater replacement requires a building permit and building inspections. That matters because a leaking valve on a tank that is already old often ends in a replacement, and the permit step applies when the tank itself is swapped. Inspections are requested online at least 24 hours ahead, and only the contractor on record schedules them.

A valve swap by itself is smaller than a replacement, and the city page does not list it. Ask Building and Safety at City Hall, 7505 S Holden Street, if you are unsure. If the tank does need replacing, ask any contractor how the permit and inspection will be handled, and read about our water heater replacement service.

Annual relief valve checklist for homeowners

A few minutes once a year catches most trouble.

  • Look at the valve for green crust, rust or white scale
  • Lift the lever and confirm water flows and stops
  • Check that the discharge pipe is present, unblocked and unthreaded
  • Measure house water pressure with a gauge, and keep it under about 80 psi
  • Confirm the thermostat is near 120 degrees
  • Note the tank's age on the label, since tanks often fail in 8 to 12 years

Can you replace the relief valve yourself?

A handy homeowner can swap the valve if the job is simple. You turn off power or gas, close the cold supply, open a hot tap and drain a few gallons below the valve, unscrew the old one, and thread in a new valve of the same rating. The risk is a mismatched valve rating, a cross-threaded fitting or a tank that is too old to disturb.

Call a plumber if the tank is more than about ten years old, if the valve is corroded in place, or if it is on a gas heater and you are not sure the control is in the right position.
