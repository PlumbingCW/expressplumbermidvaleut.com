How a hidden leak shows itself

Water does not always announce itself with a puddle. A leak inside a wall, under a slab or in a buried line shows up through side effects, and the useful part is reading them correctly.

A hissing sound at night, a damp smell in a closet, a floor that feels warm underfoot, paint that bubbles, and water pressure that drops without explanation all point to a leak. So does a meter that moves while the house is empty.

When a hidden leak becomes an emergency

Call the same day if you notice any of these, because the damage grows by the hour:

  • Water dripping from a ceiling, especially near a light fixture
  • A wet or sagging drywall wall, or a baseboard that feels soft
  • A warm spot on a slab floor, which can mean a hot water line leak
  • A sudden drop in pressure combined with a meter that will not stop
  • Sound of running water under the house while all fixtures are off
Meter and bill

Water meter still running? How to check for a leak

This test takes ten minutes, costs nothing and tells you whether a leak exists before you pay anyone.

  1. 1

    Shut everything off

    Turn off every faucet, appliance and the ice maker. Make sure nobody flushes.

  2. 2

    Read the meter

    Open the meter box. If the leak indicator or the dial moves, water is moving.

  3. 3

    Check the main

    Close the house shutoff and look again. If it stops, the leak is inside. If it keeps going, it is outside.

A toilet that runs silently is the most common false alarm, so add a few drops of food coloring to each tank and look in the bowl after 15 minutes.

High water bill and the Midvale meter system

Midvale meters every connection and reads them monthly. The city has replaced its meters with radio-read units over the past ten years, and the monthly scans flag continuous flow and unusually high use. A notice from the city or a bill that jumped for no clear reason is a reason to check, not to wait.

If your leak is between the street and the meter, or in the meter box itself, the city is the one to call, and Midvale Public Works takes water emergencies at 801-567-7235 around the clock. If the leak is on your side of the meter, we can find it.

Where it hides

Leaks in ceilings, walls, crawlspaces and yards

Each location hides a different kind of leak, and each needs a slightly different approach.

Ceiling and wall leaks

A stain below a bathroom or an upstairs supply line is the common sign. We trace the drip back to its source, since the water often travels along a joist before it shows.

Under-house and crawlspace leaks

A damp crawlspace smell, mold or pooling near piers are warning signs. We check supply lines, drains and fittings before suggesting repairs.

Slab leaks

Warm floors, the sound of water under concrete, a spike in the bill or damp carpet at the wall can all mean a slab leak. A line is usually found with sound and pressure tests before anything is cut.

Buried supply lines

A wet patch in the yard, a low-pressure complaint or a meter that keeps moving points to the line between meter and house. Galvanized steel is a common failure in older homes.

If the problem is a cracked or burst pipe, see our page on burst pipe repair for shutoff steps and what the repair involves.

Local note

Old pipe and old service lines in Midvale

Midvale's own water plan describes many distribution pipes as old, undersized and possibly leaking. The city replaces galvanized service laterals with copper or poly when they leak, and aims for about 1,000 feet of pipeline per year as budget allows.

That matters for homeowners in the older parts of town, such as Old Town, because a house with a decades-old galvanized line is more likely to leak at the connection. The portion from the meter to your house is generally your responsibility, so pressure loss or a wet yard is worth checking early.

Short notes

Irrigation, pool and yard leaks

Sprinkler lines leak at valves, risers and joints, and the usual clue is a soggy lawn patch or a pressure drop when the system starts. We locate leaks on the water supply side up to the irrigation shutoff, and an irrigation specialist handles heads and controllers.

Pool and spa plumbing is its own area. If your pool is losing water, we can test the supply and return lines, but liner and shell problems belong to a pool contractor. A meter box leak, finally, is often a failed fitting you can see standing in water, and that one may belong to the city.

Checking it yourself or calling a plumber

A few checks are free and worth doing. After that, guessing gets expensive.

You can check

  • Meter movement with everything off
  • Toilet tank dye test
  • Wet spots under sinks and around the water heater
  • Whether hot or cold side only is losing pressure

Call a licensed plumber for

  • A meter that moves with the main shutoff closed
  • Wet drywall or ceiling stains
  • Warm floors or sound under the slab
  • Any leak you cannot trace in 30 minutes

Skipping the search and cutting walls at random is the costliest way to find a leak.
