You cannot see a sewer line, and by the time it fails it is usually under a lawn, a driveway or a finished basement. A camera inspection lets you see the inside of the pipe before you buy a house, or before a slow drain becomes a backup.

Who needs one

When a sewer camera inspection is worth it

A scope is not a routine yearly chore. It earns its cost in a handful of situations.

  • You are buying a house and want to know what the lateral looks like before closing
  • A main line clog has come back after cleaning
  • Several drains gurgle or back up together
  • There are mature trees near the sewer path
  • You smell sewer gas in a bath or basement, or a floor drain has been dry for a long time
  • You plan to finish a basement or pour new concrete over the line

If only one sink is slow, a camera is rarely needed. Start with drain cleaning and look deeper only if the problem returns.

Home buyers

A sewer scope before you buy

A general home inspector looks at fixtures and visible pipes, but does not run a camera down the lateral. Replacing a failed sewer line can be one of the largest repairs a homeowner faces, so a scope during the inspection window gives you facts to negotiate with.

Ask your agent for your exact inspection deadline, because a camera visit needs a day or two of lead time. If the cleanout is buried or missing, tell us up front. We may need to pull a toilet to reach the line, which takes longer.

How the inspection works

Most scopes take under an hour once we have access.

  1. 1

    Find access

    We use a cleanout, a pulled toilet or a roof vent to get the camera into the line.

  2. 2

    Run the camera

    A flexible cable carries a lit, waterproof lens down the pipe toward the street.

  3. 3

    Mark and measure

    We note problems by distance from the entry point, and can trace the camera head above ground with a locator.

  4. 4

    Review the video

    We show you the footage, explain it in plain terms and discuss options.

Local note

Who owns the line the camera is inspecting

In Midvale, lateral maintenance and ownership typically falls on the property owner rather than the city. That means a crack found by the camera between your house and the street is usually yours to repair.

Four districts serve Midvale sewer: Midvale City Public Works, Cottonwood Improvement District, Midvalley Improvement District and Sandy Suburban Improvement District. Midvale Public Works collects wastewater for about one-third of the city and uses video inspection to find breaks and blockages. If the camera shows trouble out in the street main, we can point you to the right district.

Camera inspection or blind drain cleaning

Cleaning a line without looking can clear the symptom and miss the cause.

Cable only

  • Clears the clog for now
  • Cannot see roots, bellies or breaks
  • Clogs may return in weeks
  • No record of the pipe condition

Camera inspection

  • Shows the exact problem and its distance
  • Gives you video for a seller or insurer
  • Helps decide between cleaning, lining or repair
  • Prevents guessing on costly work

What comes after the camera

If the camera finds a cracked or collapsed section, a sewer line repair is the next conversation. If it shows heavy buildup, sewer line cleaning may be enough.

Where this guidance comes from

Camera findings are described using the International Plumbing Code (IPC) pipe and slope standards, and the same standards guide any repair that follows.
